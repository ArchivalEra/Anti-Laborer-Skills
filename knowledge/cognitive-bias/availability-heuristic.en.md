# The Availability Heuristic and Peer-Comparison Anxiety

## 1. Tversky–Kahneman Availability Heuristic

### 1.1 Core Mechanism
Tversky and Kahneman (1973) established that humans assess the frequency or probability of events not through systematic probability calculations but through the **ease with which instances can be retrieved from memory**:

> **Availability Heuristic**: When instances of a category come readily and vividly to mind, individuals overestimate both the frequency of that category and its representativeness of the broader population.

$$P_{perceived}(X) \propto \text{Ease of Retrieval}(X)$$

### 1.2 Systematic Failure in Curated Information Ecosystems
The availability heuristic is an adaptive shortcut for environments with low information complexity. In modern algorithmically mediated media environments, it fails catastrophically:
- Media that selectively presents a narrow slice of life trajectories artificially manipulates memory availability.
- Users cannot independently assess whether their perceived peer distribution represents true population statistics.

## 2. Social Media's Systematic Presentation Bias

### 2.1 Positive Survivorship Selection
Chinese social platforms (WeChat Moments, Weibo, Xiaohongshu, Douyin) and their algorithmic recommendation systems exhibit severe positive milestone selection bias:
1. **High-visibility positive milestones**: "Homeowner at 30 in Shanghai", "Entered elite firm as fresh graduate", "Promoted to VP by 28" — these generate high click-through and sharing rates, reinforcing algorithmic amplification.
2. **Suppressed negative or off-clock samples**: "Unemployed at 35", "30-year-old DINK decision", "Mid-career reorientation" — algorithmically suppressed, rarely surfaced.

**Result**: Every individual's subjective reference peer group is a positively filtered sample — **users systematically mistake presentation-biased survivorship samples for representative population distributions** when making social comparisons.

### 2.2 Festinger's Social Comparison Theory Under Algorithmic Acceleration
Leon Festinger's social comparison theory (1954) established that humans naturally evaluate themselves against "similar others." In algorithmically mediated environments, this mechanism is dramatically distorted:

- **Phantom mean elevation**: The "peer" sample presented by algorithms has a mean that significantly exceeds the true population mean, causing individuals to systematically underestimate their relative standing.
- **Comparison frequency compression**: From seasonal in agrarian societies to **real-time** in social media, triggering anxiety circuits at orders-of-magnitude higher frequency.
- **Cross-stratum illegitimate comparison**: Algorithms surface individuals from vastly different wealth levels, geographic opportunities, and structural resource endowments under the misleading label "your peers".

### 2.3 Quantitative Error Estimation
If a 25-year-old user encounters content that is 70% composed of "on-schedule milestone completion" narratives, their subjective estimate of "the proportion of peers who completed milestones on time" will be systematically overestimated by approximately 20–30 percentage points — directly converting the social clock's external normative pressure into endogenous cognitive anxiety at minimal cost to the controlling system.

## 3. Methodological Implication

The availability heuristic analysis enables a key epistemological check: when intuiting that "everyone is married/homeowning/promoted by 25," the correct question is: **Is this a population-representative statistical distribution or a survivorship-biased sample from a curated algorithmic feed?** Actual statistics on metrics like Chinese median first-marriage age (consistently rising past 28) routinely diverge from availability-heuristic-driven intuitions by 4–8 years.
